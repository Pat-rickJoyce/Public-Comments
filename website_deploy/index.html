Re: CMS-0062-P, 2026 CMS Interoperability Standards and Prior Authorization for Drugs Proposed Rule I am the CTO and co-founder of EasyPA.ai, an AI-powered prior authorization platform serving medical practices. We work daily across EMRs, clearinghouses, and payers to automate prior authorization workflows. The proposals in CMS-0062-P will materially change the infrastructure our customers and partners operate on, so I want to share field perspective on what will work, what will struggle, and what CMS can do to make this rule succeed. Overall support EasyPA.ai strongly supports the direction of this rule. Extending electronic prior authorization to drugs, adopting FHIR for HIPAA-covered PA transactions, aligning decision timeframes, and requiring specific reasons for denial are the right calls. They reduce burden on providers, improve patient access, and create the structured data foundation needed for trustworthy automation downstream. HL7v2 is not going away by October 1, 2027 CMS should plan for a hybrid reality. HL7v2 still moves the majority of clinical messages between EMRs, hospitals, labs, and pharmacies in the United States today. FHIR adoption is real and accelerating, but smaller payers, rural providers, regional plans, and many state Medicaid systems will not have rebuilt their cores in the next 17 months. They will depend on bridge infrastructure that translates between HL7v2 and FHIR in both directions. We recommend CMS explicitly acknowledge bidirectional HL7v2 to FHIR conversion as a compliant pathway, provided the FHIR endpoint exposed to trading partners conforms to the required implementation guides. Without this, smaller plans and rural providers will face binary outcomes: full rip-and-replace, or fall out of compliance. Neither serves patients. Specific reason for denial: require structured codes, not free text The requirement to provide a specific reason for denying drug PA requests is one of the most important provisions in this rule. To make it actually useful for providers and patients, CMS should require denial reasons to be expressed using structured FHIR coded values, not free-text strings. Free text cannot be reliably parsed for appeals, automated resubmission, or population-level analysis. Structured denial reasons unlock all three and create the data exhaust CMS itself will need to evaluate whether this rule is working. CDex attachments: preserve flexibility, push for structured exchange where possible Real-world PA submissions still lean heavily on PDFs, scanned documents, and faxed clinical notes. Adopting the Da Vinci CDex IG is the right move because it preserves that flexibility while creating a clean envelope. We support this. We also encourage CMS to push payers to publish, in machine-readable form, exactly which data elements they require for each PA scenario, so providers stop sending entire charts to cover unknown payer requirements. Response to RFI on payer API implementation API compliance is uneven today. Many published FHIR endpoints fail in production on issues that basic conformance testing would catch. CMS should require end-to-end testing against published test scripts, with results filed annually alongside the API usage metrics already proposed in this rule. Self-attestation alone has not produced reliable interoperability and will not produce it for drug PA either. On AI in the prior authorization pipeline CMS will receive many comments invoking artificial intelligence. The technical reality is that different AI systems do very different things in this workflow. Classification models triage and route. Generative models draft appeals and summarize clinical documentation. These are distinct capabilities with distinct risk profiles. Any future rulemaking on AI in prior authorization should make this distinction explicit and require transparency on which model types are used at each decision point, particularly when AI contributes to a denial. Closing The October 1, 2027 compliance date is achievable for payers that begin work now and that can access interoperability infrastructure built on open standards. EasyPA.ai is committed to supporting that transition through purpose-built tooling, including our HL7v2 to FHIR converter available on AWS Marketplace. Thank you for the opportunity to comment. Respectfully, Alex Vega CTO, EasyPA.ai

Timely access to care is essential for people living with ALS, a rapidly progressive disease that requires prompt and uninterrupted medical intervention. Delays in authorizing medically necessary services, equipment, or medications can result in irreversible loss of function, increased caregiver burden, and avoidable health complications. The ALS Association urges CMS to finalize this rule with strong, enforceable protections to safeguard timely and nondiscriminatory access to care. This includes adopting default expedited pathways for ALS‑related services, shortening decision timelines for urgent requests, and supporting utilization management approaches—such as appropriately structured gold‑carding programs—that recognize established provider expertise. Together, these reforms will reduce treatment disruptions, promote effective care coordination, and align utilization management with the realities of progressive and life‑limiting conditions. In closing, the ALS Association strongly supports CMS’s efforts to modernize prior authorization and urges continued improvements that prioritize access, efficiency, and equity for medically complex patients. By implementing these reforms, CMS can strengthen the health care system’s ability to serve individuals with critical medical needs, improve the delivery of ALS care, and ensure that coverage policies support timely, patient centered treatment. We sincerely appreciate CMS’s dedication to addressing the unique challenges faced by people living with ALS and recognizing their urgent healthcare needs.

I am submitting this comment as a prior authorization and FHIR interoperability consultant with direct experience supporting payers, providers, and health-tech teams navigating CMS interoperability requirements and Da Vinci implementation work. I support the core objective of CMS-0062-P — extending electronic prior authorization requirements to drugs and shortening decision timeframes. The operational case for this direction is well-established. Manual drug prior authorization workflows create avoidable delays in medication access, drive unnecessary administrative burden, and produce inconsistent denial rationale that makes appeals difficult for providers and patients alike. Extending the electronic prior authorization framework to drugs is the right direction. However I want to offer operationally grounded comments on three specific aspects of the proposed rule that I believe require further consideration before finalization. DECISION TIMEFRAME FEASIBILITY The proposed compression of standard drug prior authorization decisions to 72 hours and urgent decisions to 24 hours is significantly more aggressive than the current non-drug PA requirements. I support shorter timeframes as a goal. My concern is sequencing. Organizations that have not yet completed workflow redesign for non-drug PA — which represents a substantial portion of affected payers — will face compounded implementation pressure. I recommend CMS consider a phased approach that allows payers to demonstrate operational readiness at the non-drug PA standard before the drug PA timeframes take effect. Compliance with a timeline that outpaces operational capacity produces paper compliance, not genuine improvement in patient access. WORKFLOW DESIGN BEFORE API REQUIREMENT The most significant implementation failure pattern I observe across prior authorization modernization efforts is the assumption that API compliance produces operational results. It does not. A FHIR Prior Authorization API built on top of broken intake, documentation, routing, and status workflows produces a compliant system that still delivers poor operational performance. I urge CMS to strengthen guidance around workflow readiness requirements alongside the technical API specifications — specifically around intake completeness, documentation alignment, and denial rationale generation. Technical conformance testing through Inferno validates API endpoints. It does not validate that those endpoints are connected to workflows capable of meeting the proposed decision timeframes. HUMAN ACCOUNTABILITY IN AI-ASSISTED DRUG PA As AI-assisted prior authorization tools are increasingly deployed to support the shorter decision timeframes this rule proposes, I urge CMS to strengthen its guidance on human accountability requirements for drug PA decisions. Drug prior authorization decisions directly affect patient access to medication — with immediate health consequences when delays occur. ONC's HTI-1 Final Rule is establishing algorithm transparency requirements for AI in certified health IT. CMS-0062-P should explicitly align with that direction by requiring that AI-assisted drug PA workflows maintain documented human accountability at the decision point. Automated denial of a drug prior authorization request without human review creates both patient safety risk and compliance exposure that the proposed rule should address directly. I appreciate CMS's continued leadership on prior authorization modernization and support the overall direction of this proposed rule. The comments above are offered in the interest of ensuring that the final rule produces genuine operational improvement in patient access — not just technical compliance. Respectfully submitted, Pete Moses MosesAI Consulting Prior Authorization and FHIR Interoperability Consultant

Calvient is a healthcare technology company that develops AI-powered solutions for prior authorizations, referrals, intelligent document processing, fax management, and healthcare operations automation. We serve federally qualified health centers (FQHCs), community health centers, and health systems across the United States. We strongly support CMS-0062-P and the extension of electronic prior authorization requirements to prescription drugs. The compressed decision timeframes (24-hour urgent, 72-hour standard), adoption of FHIR and NCPDP standards, specific denial reason requirements, and public reporting of PA metrics are all essential reforms. Our detailed comments are provided in the attached document. We wish to highlight one central point: the fax is not the problem. While electronic transmission standards are necessary, the true burden lies in the intensity, variability, and volume of payer-specific documentation requirements. Providers at FQHCs and health systems dedicate full-time staff solely to completing PA questionnaires, scrubbing patient charts for relevant clinical data, and monitoring submission status. Moving from fax to API makes submission faster but does not reduce this preparatory work. This is why the Da Vinci Documentation Templates and Rules (DTR) implementation guide is the most consequential element of this rule. DTR enables payers to deliver documentation requirements in structured, machine-readable formats that can be pre-populated with clinical data from the patient record. We urge CMS to mandate DTR adoption with the same urgency as PAS. We also urge CMS to recognize the emerging role of AI-augmented workflows in prior authorization. AI agents are already assisting providers by scrubbing charts, pre-populating questionnaire responses, verifying PA necessity, and monitoring submission status. These tools do not replace human clinical judgment, but rather augment human capabilities and efficiency. The EPA framework established by this rule should be designed to facilitate AI-assisted workflows by ensuring DTR questionnaires are machine-processable, PAS APIs support programmatic status queries, and payer API access policies accommodate technology-assisted throughput. Our attached comment provides detailed recommendations including: mandating DTR with equal urgency to PAS, requiring PA necessity determination via API, establishing questionnaire consistency standards, designing EPA for AI-augmented workflows, and addressing the unique needs of FQHCs. We also respond to the RFIs on step therapy, laboratory/DME PA burden, and cybersecurity. Calvient welcomes the opportunity to serve as a resource to CMS on this rulemaking.

PUBLIC COMMENT ON CMS-0062-P Docket: CMS-2026-1255-0001 RFI Addressed: III.C. Improving Implementation of Payer API Technology Submitted by: Akken Yakupitiyage, Founder, Zkeleton This is a summary. The full comment is in the attached PDF. I am the founder of Zkeleton, a pre-seed company building clinical data normalization infrastructure for health plans. Zkeleton is not a regulated entity under this rule, does not currently sell into the impacted payer categories, and has no direct commercial position in the outcome. The proposed rule meaningfully advances interoperability by extending API requirements to drugs and establishing an endpoint registry. The most important unaddressed risk is the gap between technical conformance to FHIR implementation guides and the practical usability of the data those guides convey. THE CONFORMANCE GAP Conformance ensures that data moves. Fidelity ensures that data arrives in a form the receiving payer can act on. The current testing framework, including ONC Inferno, validates the first but not the second. Five patterns illustrate the gap: 1. Unstructured attachments under CDex. Two payers can pass conformance while one transmits structured Observations with LOINC codes and the other transmits a scanned PDF of the same lab. 2. Code system fragmentation. Prior treatment history may appear as RxNorm RXCUIs, NDCs, internal payer codes, or free text. All conformant; only the first supports automated decisioning. 3. Semantic loss across Payer-to-Payer. A member who cleared step therapy at one payer may be forced to repeat it at the next, because the receiving payer cannot operationalize the prior failure history even though it was technically transmitted. 4. Absence of round-trip scenario testing. No widely adopted test verifies that a clinically meaningful PA scenario can be transmitted, received, and rendered into a coverage decision without semantic loss. 5. Identity and demographic resolution. Patient matching across payers is governed by each payer's internal algorithms, with no rule-level expectations for matching quality or transparency. RECOMMENDATIONS 1. Establish semantic fidelity benchmarks alongside conformance testing, covering structured-Observation rates, RxNorm-encoding rates, and structured-rationale rates for PA decisions. Publishing them is itself a market signal. 2. Require structured representation when the source representation is structured. PDF wrapping of natively structured data should be discouraged through the testing and certification framework. 3. Develop a reference test corpus for round-trip scenarios covering specialty drugs with prior step therapy, oncology with biomarker-based eligibility, transplant immunosuppressants, and autoimmune biologics. 4. Publish a data fidelity score alongside the API endpoint registry described in Section II.E, so receiving payers, providers, and patients can assess upstream quality and payers have a market incentive to invest in fidelity. 5. Clarify that nothing in the conformance and testing framework prohibits payers from staging, normalizing, or quality-checking ingested clinical data in segregated or single-tenant processing environments before promoting it to operational systems. This clarification removes ambiguity for payer privacy, security, and compliance teams considering modern data architectures. TRADEOFFS Three tradeoffs are worth naming. First, fidelity benchmarks add cost and could slow small payer adoption; CMS should consider phasing them twelve to eighteen months after conformance compliance. Second, premature standardization is a real risk; the recommendations above are framed as benchmarks rather than fixed mandates to allow evolution. Third, burden on small entities is real and should be mitigated through delayed timelines or modified expectations for smaller payers. WHY THIS MATTERS FOR PATIENTS The drug prior authorization extension addresses care categories where delay translates directly into harm: specialty drugs, oncology regimens, autoimmune biologics. A patient who has cleared step therapy at one plan should not be forced to repeat it at the next because the receiving payer cannot operationalize the prior failure history. Conformance moves the data. Fidelity ensures it can be used. The attached PDF contains the full analysis of each gap and recommendation. I appreciate the opportunity to comment and would welcome follow-up from CMS staff on the technical specifics. Respectfully submitted, Akken Yakupitiyage Founder, Zkeleton akkenyaku@zkeleton.com

Dear CMS, I am a technology leader and IEEE Senior Member with experience supporting AI adoption across 180 or more organizations in healthcare, enterprise, and public sector environments. My peer reviewed research on secure AI architectures for healthcare critical infrastructure was published at IEEE SoutheastCon 2026, and I have written for Healthcare IT Today on post deployment AI governance. I am submitting this comment based on practical experience with AI driven automation in regulated healthcare environments. My comments focus on sections II.B (Electronic Prior Authorization for Drugs) and II.C (Improving Communications and Decision Timeframes for Prior Authorizations). 1. Prior authorization decisions that involve AI need transparency at the decision layer Prior authorization for drugs is increasingly automated on the payer side. AI is used to triage, recommend, or deny requests before a human ever looks at the file. When AI contributes to a denial or an adverse determination, the specific reason for denial requirements in this rule should extend to the AI layer itself. That means disclosing the model version in use at the time of the decision, the inputs it considered, and whether a human actually reviewed the determination before it was sent out. The proposed public reporting of denial and appeal metrics is a strong step. Without transparency at the decision layer, though, those metrics will describe the outcome but not how the outcome was produced. Patients, prescribers, and pharmacists receive the answer without the decision process behind it, which limits what any of them can do with the information. 2. FHIR based Prior Authorization API requirements should be paired with AI metadata The FHIR first direction of this rule, including the proposed adoption of the CRD, DTR, and PAS implementation guides for the Prior Authorization API, is strong. As payers and providers exchange data to support electronic prior authorization for drugs covered under a medical benefit, the data envelope should carry enough metadata to identify when AI was used in a decision path. Existing FHIR resources can be extended to carry this metadata without a redesign of the implementation guides CMS is proposing to require. This is consistent with where HTI-5 was heading on automated actor recognition and with broader federal AI governance work under the NIST AI RMF. Carrying AI metadata in the same transactions that already carry prior authorization decisions avoids standing up a parallel disclosure channel later. 3. Vendor accountability applies to AI enabled prior authorization Many impacted payers deploy AI for prior authorization through third party vendors, including pharmacy benefit managers and utilization management vendors. The contracts with those vendors were usually written for traditional rules engines, not adaptive AI. Model updates, training data provenance, and ongoing performance monitoring typically are not covered. As CMS establishes the October 1, 2027 compliance dates, the electronic prior authorization requirements, and the decision timeframes in this rule, it is worth addressing vendor accountability for the AI components of the decision pipeline. Otherwise the impacted payer carries the regulatory burden for behavior it does not fully control, and the provider has no clear entity to engage with when something goes wrong. 4. Continuous monitoring of denial and appeal patterns The proposed public reporting requirements for prior authorization for drugs would be stronger with a continuous monitoring expectation built in. Impacted payers could track and report on denial rates, appeal reversal rates, and decision timing by drug category and patient population. That kind of monitoring creates an operational feedback loop that helps surface when AI driven systems are producing unintended patterns, rather than waiting for those patterns to show up in complaints, state enforcement, or litigation. It also aligns with the direction the proposed numeric count metrics are already moving in. Thank you for the opportunity to contribute. As a healthcare leader responsible for clinical informatics and operational workflows, I strongly support the intent of this proposed rule to modernize prior authorization through improved interoperability and electronic processes. Prior authorization delays are not simply administrative inefficiencies—they directly impact patient care. When approvals are delayed, patients experience postponed treatments, interruptions in medication access, and, in some cases, worsening clinical outcomes. For this reason, the proposed decision timeframes are not just operational benchmarks; they are critical patient safety safeguards. However, the effectiveness of this rule will depend on meaningful enforcement. If payers are not held accountable to consistently meet these timelines, the burden of delay will continue to fall on patients despite the transition to electronic systems. Additionally, it is essential that payers bear the primary responsibility for the costs and operational lift associated with integration and implementation. Prior authorization requirements originate from payer policies, and the administrative complexity that this rule seeks to address has largely been driven by payer processes. While providers will need to interact with enhanced digital workflows, shifting the cost burden onto health systems would further strain organizations already managing significant clinical and financial pressures. True reform requires clear accountability—both in terms of adherence to required timelines and investment in the infrastructure needed to support interoperability. Without enforceable standards and appropriate allocation of responsibility, there is a risk that these changes will not achieve their intended impact. Ensuring payer accountability will be key to delivering meaningful improvements in efficiency, transparency, and, most importantly, patient care.
