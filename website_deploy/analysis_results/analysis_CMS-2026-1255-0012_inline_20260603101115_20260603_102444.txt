Hello,

I am submitting this comment as an undergraduate student pursuing dual degrees in Public Health and Mathematical Data Science at San Diego State University, where I am also involved in public health research focused on infectious disease modeling, community health impact assessment, and health equity in underserved border communities.

I strongly support the proposed rule's expansion of interoperability standards and electronic prior authorization (ePA) requirements to Medicare Advantage organizations, Medicaid managed care plans, and CHIP agencies. This rule addresses a critical bottleneck in the healthcare system, one that disproportionately harms the most vulnerable patients.

Prior authorization delays are not "administrative inconveniences". Research has consistently shown that PA burdens fall hardest on Medicaid beneficiaries, who are more likely to be patients of color, low-income, and reliant on community health centers with limited administrative capacity to manage PA workflows. In border communities like those along the San Diego-Tijuana corridor, where I have conducted community health research, these barriers compound existing access inequities. A patient waiting days or weeks for PA approval on a necessary medication may forgo care entirely with downstream consequences for chronic disease management, emergency utilization, and population health outcomes.

The proposed FHIR-based standards for drug PA are a meaningful step toward reducing this burden. Standardized electronic PA workflows would reduce the time providers spend on administrative tasks, accelerate decisions for patients, and generate structured data that public health researchers and policymakers can use to monitor disparities in PA approval rates across demographic groups and plan types.

However, I urge CMS to go further in two areas:

First, the proposed rule should require plans to publicly report PA approval and denial rates, stratified by race, ethnicity, language, and condition type. Without this data transparency, it is impossible to identify and correct discriminatory patterns in PA decision-making. The rule's interoperability infrastructure makes this reporting technically feasible — CMS should require it.

Second, I urge CMS to set meaningful timelines for drug PA decisions that reflect clinical urgency. The proposed rule is an opportunity to establish enforceable standards that protect patients when delays can cause measurable harm.

I commend CMS for advancing this rulemaking and urge the agency to finalize it with strong equity provisions. Health data infrastructure is only as valuable as the health outcomes it produces, and this rule has the potential to meaningfully reduce disparities if implemented with that goal in mind.

Thank you for the opportunity to comment.
