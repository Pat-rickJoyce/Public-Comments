## 2. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Julia Lima, an RN and healthcare entrepreneur, supports the rule's PA timeframes and FHIR foundation but identifies a critical unaddressed gap: PA decisions are only as sound as the underlying clinical data, which is routinely fragmented at care transitions. She urges CMS to treat the patient's complete clinical record as infrastructure that must travel with the patient across the continuum, so that ePA decisions are based on current, complete data—not just made faster.

---

## 3. REGULATORY CONTEXT

- **Specific provisions addressed:** PA-DRUG-MEDICAL, PA-TIMEFRAMES-DRUGS, FHIR-IG-REQUIRED
- **Regulatory citations mentioned:** None specific to CFR
- **Standards referenced:** HL7 FHIR; National Patient Safety Goal NPSG.03.06.01 (Joint Commission)

---

## 4. DETAILED POSITIONS

**Topic 1: Support for PA Timeframes and FHIR Foundation**
- **Provision ID:** PA-TIMEFRAMES-DRUGS, FHIR-IG-REQUIRED, PA-DRUG-MEDICAL
- **Position:** Support
- **Rationale:** A 24-hour deadline for urgent requests and 72 hours for standard requests will prevent harm. FHIR is the correct technical foundation. Extending PA API to medical benefit drugs closes a real gap.
- **Specific request:** None; general affirmation.

**Topic 2: Incomplete Clinical Record as Root Cause of PA Decision Quality Gap**
- **Provision ID:** PA-DRUG-MEDICAL, GENERAL
- **Position:** Modify (broaden scope)
- **Rationale:** The rule standardizes the PA transaction but assumes the underlying clinical record is complete and current. Literature documents that 27–54% of patients have medication history discrepancies at hospital admission; 85% of admission medication errors originated in the medication history (MATCH study); medication reconciliation failures are a persistent patient safety problem. A faster PA pathway built on incomplete data delivers a faster decision, not a safer one.
- **Specific request:** CMS should address the completeness and currency of the underlying clinical record, not just the speed of the PA transaction. In this and future rulemakings, treat the patient's complete clinical record as infrastructure that must move with the patient across the continuum of care.

---

## 5. SOLICITATION RESPONSES

- **S12:** Lima implicitly responds by noting that technical challenges in medical benefit drug PA extend beyond transaction standards to the quality of clinical data submitted—specifically medication reconciliation failures at care transitions.
- **S20:** Lima supports the 24-hour urgency timeframe but cautions that speed of decision does not equal quality of decision when the underlying clinical record is incomplete; the timeline measures latency rather than decision accuracy.
- **S21:** Not directly addressed; Lima's concern is data quality, not timeline feasibility per se.

---

## 6. RECOMMENDATIONS

1. CMS should address the completeness and currency of underlying clinical records feeding PA requests, not just the speed of the PA transaction.
2. In this and future rulemakings, treat the patient's complete clinical record as infrastructure that must accompany the patient across all care settings and transitions.
3. CMS staff should engage with bedside clinicians to understand medication handoff failures in detail.

---

## 7. CONCERNS & OBJECTIONS

- **Primary concerns:** PA decisions will be faster but not necessarily safer; the rule assumes complete, reconciled, current clinical records at the point of submission—an assumption that rarely holds in practice.
- **Potential negative impacts cited:** Adverse drug events arising from PA approvals based on incomplete medication histories; the rule optimizes transaction latency without improving decision quality.
- **Implementation barriers mentioned:** Medication history discrepancies at transitions (27–54% of patients); records not transmitted or not parseable by receiving EHR; clinical identity of patients fragments across prehospital, ED, SNF, pharmacy, and home settings.

---

## 8. SUPPORTING EVIDENCE

- **Data or statistics cited:** 27–54% of patients carry at least one medication history discrepancy at hospital admission; 19–75% of those discrepancies unintentional; 85% of admission medication errors originated in medication history; nearly half were omissions (MATCH study); IOM estimated preventable adverse drug events injure at least 1.5 million Americans per year.
- **Studies or research referenced:** Marinović et al. (2016), Croat Med J (medication reconciliation at admission); Gleason et al. (2010), J Gen Intern Med (MATCH study); Joint Commission National Patient Safety Goals (NPSG.03.06.01); Institute of Medicine (2006), Preventing Medication Errors.
- **Examples provided:** Patient arriving by ambulance, obtunded, with no reliable history and partial/unverified medication and allergy records; transferring facility records not transmitted or not parseable by receiving EHR.

---

## 9. IMPACT ASSESSMENT

- **Affected stakeholders:** Patients (especially at care transitions), bedside clinicians, payers, EHR systems, pharmacies, SNFs
- **Economic implications:** Not directly addressed; implicit cost from adverse drug events
- **Technical feasibility:** The FHIR transaction layer is seen as feasible and correct; the gap is clinical data completeness at transitions, not transaction technology
- **Patient care impact:** High concern — faster PA decisions based on incomplete data can accelerate approval of clinically inappropriate therapies, contributing to the 1.5 million annual preventable adverse drug events
