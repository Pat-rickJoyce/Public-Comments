ORIGINAL COMMENT EXCERPT
------------------------
That prior authorization requires severe reform can be seen from even a cursory overview of its public-facing process.  Requirements for authorization of tests or treatments are not standardized even within a specific insurance company, differing from one plan to the next.  It&rsquo;s impossible to know or look up criteria for innumerable tests and treatments.  Companies use phone-tree and call center gatekeepers who shield decision-makers from being contacted.  Documentation is burdensome and done by cumbersome faxing.  When portals exist, they are clunky and difficult to use, and the physician, who may lack authority to sign a contract within an organization, is forced to sign one in order to get access.  Denials are algorithmic and automatic in many cases, such that appeal is the only way to seek approval, putting massive stress on physicians and nurses, and inevitably delaying the process.  Reviewers are not of the same specialty as the requesting physician, and reviews aren&rsquo;t substantive, as the reviewers are required to restrict their opinions to plan language not the best interests of the patient.  Every time Earth circles the Sun, the annual review puts the patient at risk for interruption of therapy.  There&rsquo;s no way for physicians to speak with anyone who has decision making authority, even upon urgent demand.  The structure of the prior authorization system alone is diagnostic of a bad faith process.  One doesn&rsquo;t even have to delve into the details of what&rsquo;s covered and what&rsquo;s not in order to expose prior authorization as a crude process designed to deter medical care, and to undo whatever a physician has already done.  It&rsquo;s not even necessary to recount the harms done to patients by this process to know that it&rsquo;s a heartless cost control mechanism.  This is my personal opinion, not that of any organization....
